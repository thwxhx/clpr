PART A – QUICK NOTES

Module 1: Design Thinking Basics

  • Design thinking = human-centred, iterative, problem-solving approach: understand users → frame problem → generate ideas → build → test.
  • 5 stages: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test (non-linear, repeat).
  • Double Diamond (British Design Council, 2005): Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver. 1st diamond = right problem; 2nd = right solution. Each: diverge then converge.
  • Concept from functions: overall function → sub-functions → function structure → find solution principle for each → combine.
  • Brainstorming (Alex Osborn): group idea-generation. Rules: defer judgement, quantity over quality, wild ideas welcome, build on others’ ideas, one conversation at a time. Steps: state problem → generate ideas → record all → group → evaluate & select.
  • Creativity ↑ by: diverse teams, mind-maps, analogy, constraints, breaks, no fear of failure, and SCAMPER = Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange.

Module 2: Design Process

  • Requirements: identify customer needs (survey, interview, observation) → market analysis (competitors, trends) → define goals/specifications.
  • Product concepts: establish functions + task specifications (what product must do, measurable limits).
  • Solution concept: conceptualise → generate alternatives → evaluate → select.
  • Embodiment design: give concept physical form – layout, shape, material, dimensions; then analysis, optimisation, experiment, marketing.
  • Human-centred design (HCD): users in the centre at every stage – understand context, specify needs, design, evaluate with users.

Module 3: Evaluation & Prototyping

  • Pugh matrix: compare each concept with a datum (reference concept, scored 0 on all criteria) as + (better), S/0 (same), − (worse) per criterion. Qualitative, used at early stage.
  • Decision matrix: weighted criteria × ratings → total score.
  • Prototype: early, rough, working model to test an idea. Why: test, communicate, find flaws early, save cost.
  • Proof of concept (PoC): shows the idea is technically feasible. Model = representation (physical/virtual/mathematical).
  • Rapid prototyping: fast build (3D printing, CNC, laser cutting) in hours/days.
  • Lean startup: Build → Measure → Learn using MVP (minimum viable product), pivot or persevere.

Module 4: Ethics & Case Studies

  • 4.1 Ethics in design: moral principles that guide engineers’ decisions. Obligations to: public (safety, health, welfare), client/employer (honesty, confidentiality), profession (integrity, competence), environment (sustainability).
  • Code of ethics = written rules of professional conduct; gives guidance, protects public, builds trust, helps in dilemmas.
  • ASCE (American Society of Civil Engineers) canons: public safety, health & welfare paramount; practise only in area of competence; issue truthful public statements; act as faithful agent of employer; avoid deception; uphold honour of profession; support sustainable development.
  • IEEE code: hold public safety paramount; disclose factors that endanger public/environment; avoid real or perceived conflict of interest; be honest in claims and estimates; reject bribery; improve technical competence; seek and accept honest criticism; credit others’ work; avoid injuring others; treat all persons fairly; help colleagues.
  • VDI (Verein Deutscher Ingenieure, German engineers’ association): ethical principles of engineering profession; guideline VDI 3780 (technology assessment) values = functionality, economic efficiency, safety, health, environmental quality, personality development, social quality.
  • Moral frameworks: Utilitarian (greatest good for greatest number); Rights-based (respect people’s rights); Duty/deontological (follow rules and duties); Virtue ethics (act as a person of good character); Justice/fairness (equal treatment).
  • Ethical dilemma = conflict between obligations (e.g., cost cut vs safety). Resolve: identify facts → stakeholders → options → apply code/framework → decide → review. Whistle-blowing if employer ignores a public danger.
  • Failures from ignoring ethics: Bhopal gas tragedy (1984, safety cut for cost), Ford Pinto (fuel-tank risk known), Challenger shuttle (warnings ignored).
  • 4.2 Challenges in design thinking:
    • Truly empathising: users often cannot state needs; observer bias; small samples.
    • Time and cost of repeated iterations; needs commitment from management.
    • Subjective, hard-to-measure results (difficult to prove return on investment).
    • Team issues: poor collaboration, conflicting views, lack of diversity or trained facilitators.
    • Organisation: resistance to change, rigid hierarchy, fear of failure.
    • Balancing desirability (user), feasibility (technology), viability (business); scaling prototype to mass production; ethical and environmental limits.
  • Overcome by: clear goals, user testing, cross-functional teams, training, time-boxed iterations, management support.
  • 4.3 Design thinking case studies – study these aspects: user and problem → empathy findings → problem definition → ideas generated → prototype → testing → result/impact → lessons learnt.
  • Examples: Airbnb (observed hosts, improved listing photos → bookings rose); Embrace low-cost infant warmer (designed for rural, no-electricity settings); Apple mouse / IDEO shopping cart (team-based rapid prototyping).

PART B – EQUATIONS (every term explained)

1. Decision matrix (weighted score)

Total Score Sj = Σ (Wi × Rij)   i = 1 … n

  • Sj – final weighted score of concept j (higher = better).
  • Σ – sum over all criteria.
  • Wi – weight (importance) of criterion i, e.g. cost 30%, safety 40%. Σ Wi = 100% (or 1).
  • Rij – rating of concept j against criterion i (e.g. 1–5 scale).
  • i – criterion number; n – number of criteria; j – concept number.

Concept A selected (3.8 > 3.6).

2. Pugh’s evaluation matrix

Net score = Σ(+) − Σ(−)

  • Σ(+) – number of criteria where the concept is better than datum.
  • Σ(−) – number where it is worse. Σ(0) – same as datum.
  • Datum – reference concept (usually existing product/best concept) scored 0 everywhere.
  • Highest net score → best; then improve it by combining good features of other concepts.
  • Weighted Pugh: Net score = Σ (Wi × scorei), where scorei = +1, 0 or −1 and Wi = weight of criterion i.

3. Weighting factor (normalised)

Wi = (Importance rating of i) / (Σ all importance ratings)

Converts raw importance (1–10) into fractions that add up to 1.

4. Lean startup loop

Build (MVP) → Measure (data) → Learn (pivot/persevere)

PART C – CO ASSESSMENT ANSWERS (5 marks each)

CO1

Q1. Describe design thinking and list the stages.

  1. Human-centred, creative, iterative method to solve complex/ill-defined problems.
  2. Empathize – observe/interview users to understand feelings and needs.
  3. Define – state the problem (point-of-view statement) clearly.
  4. Ideate – brainstorm many ideas, choose promising ones.
  5. Prototype – build quick low-cost models. Test – get user feedback, refine; loop back as needed.

Q2. Illustrate the Double Diamond model.

  1. Developed by British Design Council (2005); two diamonds, each = diverge (open) then converge (focus).
  2. Discover (diverge) – research, insights into the problem.
  3. Define (converge) – synthesise insights into clear design brief.
  4. Develop (diverge) – ideas, concepts, prototypes, co-design.
  5. Deliver (converge) – test, finalise, launch. (Draw: two diamonds side by side, labelled Problem | Solution, with 4 stage names below.)

Q3. Develop concepts from functions & mechanism of brainstorming.

  1. Concept from functions: state overall function (black box: input → output).
  2. Break into sub-functions → draw function structure.
  3. Find working principles/solutions for each sub-function (morphological chart) → combine into concept variants.
  4. Brainstorming: group states problem, facilitator, 15–30 min; every idea recorded, no criticism (defer judgement), wild ideas welcome, build on ideas, aim for quantity.
  5. Afterwards group, evaluate, and select ideas (voting).

Q4. Increase creativity and form ideas from conception to implementation.

  1. Increase creativity: SCAMPER (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse), mind-mapping, analogies, diverse team, open environment, set constraints.
  2. Conception: problem → need identified → idea generated.
  3. Concept development & evaluation (select best).
  4. Detail design, prototype, test.
  5. Implementation: manufacture, launch, collect feedback, improve.

CO2

Q1. Narrow down to best design (needs, market, goals).

  1. Collect customer needs (surveys, interviews, observation); rank them.
  2. Market analysis – competitors, price, trends, gaps.
  3. Convert needs into measurable specifications/goals.
  4. Generate alternatives; screen using criteria.
  5. Evaluate with Pugh/decision matrix → select the best that meets needs, cost & market.

Q2. Process of product concepts, forming ideas and embodiment design.

  1. Establish functions and task specifications (requirement list).
  2. Generate ideas for each function; combine into concepts.
  3. Evaluate and select solution concept.
  4. Embodiment design: layout, form, material, dimensions, standard parts.
  5. Analyse & optimise (strength, cost, weight), experiment/test, then marketing and detail design.

Q3. Explain the Human-centred design process.

  1. Design focused on people who will use the product; they are involved throughout.
  2. Understand and specify context of use.
  3. Specify user requirements.
  4. Produce design solutions (prototypes).
  5. Evaluate with users, iterate until needs are met. Result: usable, safe, accessible products.

CO3

Q1. Concept evaluation – Pugh’s matrix & decision matrix with examples.

  1. Pugh: list criteria; choose datum (reference concept); mark each concept + (better), 0/S (same), − (worse) against datum; net score = Σ(+) − Σ(−).
  2. Eg: Chair concepts vs datum on cost, comfort, weight: A = +,+,− → net +1.
  3. Decision matrix: assign weights W to criteria, rate each concept R; S = Σ(W×R).
  4. Example: A = 3.8, B = 3.6 (see Part B) → A selected.
  5. Pugh = qualitative, quick, early stage; decision matrix = quantitative, later stage. Do sensitivity check.

Q2. Ideation of prototypes, models and proofs of concept.

  1. Model: representation of product (physical, digital CAD, mathematical).
  2. Prototype: working/early sample to test form, fit and function.
  3. Proof of concept: checks idea is technically feasible; may not look like final product.
  4. Ideation: sketches → mock-ups → functional prototype.
  5. Benefits: find flaws early, communicate ideas, reduce risk and cost.

Q3. Rapid prototyping, lean startup, testing of prototypes.

  1. Rapid prototyping: fast making of parts from CAD – 3D printing, CNC, laser cutting.
  2. Lean startup: Build MVP → Measure customer response → Learn (pivot or persevere).
  3. Avoids waste, fast feedback, low cost.
  4. Testing: define test goals, test with real users, record observations.
  5. Refine and re-test until concept is proven.

CO4

Q1. How ethics play a decisive role in design.

  1. Engineers have obligations to public safety, health and welfare (ASCE, IEEE, VDI).
  2. Code of ethics: honesty, competence, confidentiality, avoid conflict of interest, no bribery.
  3. Ethical design considers safety, environment, privacy, fairness, sustainability.
  4. Examples of failures: Bhopal gas, Ford Pinto – ignored ethics → harm and loss.
  5. Moral frameworks (utilitarian, rights, duty, virtue) guide hard decisions.

Q2. Challenges in design thinking.

  1. Difficult to truly empathise / user bias; users can’t always state needs.
  2. Time-consuming and costly iterations.
  3. Hard to measure results; subjective evaluation.
  4. Team conflicts, resistance to change, lack of skilled people.
  5. Scaling prototype to mass production; balancing desirability, feasibility, viability.

Q3. Functional structure of a shopping cart.

  1. Overall function: carry goods conveniently (input: goods + user push → output: goods moved).
  2. Sub-functions: hold goods (basket), support (frame), move (wheels), steer/push (handle), carry child (seat).
  3. Further: nest/store (stackable), lock/secure (coin lock), brake/stop (castor lock).
  4. Flow: user force → handle → frame → wheels → motion; goods → basket → frame.
  5. Draw as block diagram with energy, material, signal flows.

Q4. Changes in bag design – cost, reliability, production, environment.

  1. Cost: cheaper fabric, fewer parts, simple stitching, standard zips.
  2. Reliability: reinforced seams/straps, strong zip, load-test, water-resistant lining.
  3. Production: modular panels, automated cutting, fewer operations, easy assembly.
  4. Environment: recycled/biodegradable fabrics, less waste, long life, repairable, recyclable.
  5. Trade-offs balanced using decision matrix.

PART D – CASE STUDY TOOLKIT

Write in this order (works for ANY case):
  1. Problem statement: “The problem is to design … for [user] who needs … because …”
  2. Empathize: “Users were studied through interviews, surveys and observation to understand pain points.”
  3. Define: “Requirements were converted into measurable specifications – cost, safety, size, durability, ease of use.”
  4. Ideate: “Brainstorming and function decomposition generated multiple alternative concepts.”
  5. Evaluate: “Concepts were compared using Pugh’s matrix and weighted decision matrix; the highest scoring concept was selected.”
  6. Prototype & test: “A low-cost prototype/MVP was built and tested with real users, and refined iteratively.”
  7. Ethics & environment: “The design follows codes of ethics (ASCE/IEEE/VDI), prioritises public safety, and uses sustainable materials.”
  8. Conclusion: “Applying the human-centred, iterative design thinking process resulted in a feasible, desirable and viable solution.”

Power phrases (use anywhere)

  • “Human-centred and iterative approach”
  • “Balances desirability, feasibility and viability”
  • “Diverge to generate options, converge to select”
  • “Fail early, fail cheap, learn fast”
  • “Trade-off between cost, reliability, manufacturability and environment”
  • “Stakeholder feedback incorporated at every stage”
  • “Challenges: time, cost, user bias – mitigated by iteration and testing”
